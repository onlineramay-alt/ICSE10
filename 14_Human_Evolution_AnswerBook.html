C. Short Answer

Q22. The vermiform appendix projects from the blind end of the caecum (a part of the large intestine) as a narrow, worm-like tube. In humans, it is a functionless (vestigial) organ, even though its corresponding organ in herbivorous mammals (e.g. sheep) is large and helps digest cellulose — making the human appendix a vestige of a once-functional ancestral structure.

Official Answer

Q23. Lamarck proposed that organs which are used continually and extensively become larger, stronger and more developed over an organism's lifetime, while organs that are not used gradually weaken and degenerate ("use and disuse"). He further proposed that such acquired changes are passed on to the offspring, so that over many generations, the used organ becomes progressively more developed in the species (illustrated by his classic giraffe's-neck example).

Q26. This is false. Bat wings and butterfly wings are ANALOGOUS organs, not homologous — they perform the same function (flight) but have completely different origins and structures: a bat's wing is a modified vertebrate forelimb, while a butterfly's wing is an outgrowth of its exoskeleton (cuticle). Since they do not share a common evolutionary/structural origin, they cannot be called homologous.

Official Answer

Q27. Speciation is the process by which one species gives rise to one or more new, distinct species over time. In geographical (allopatric) speciation, a large, widely-distributed population becomes divided by a physical barrier (river, mountain, ocean) into isolated sub-populations, which accumulate different variations and eventually become reproductively isolated, new species. In sympatric speciation, new species arise within the same geographical area, without physical separation, through other forms of reproductive isolation (e.g. differences in breeding behaviour or timing).
