A. Multiple Choice Questions

Q1. (c) depends on the material

Specific latent heat is the heat absorbed or released per unit mass during a change of state at constant temperature. It is a characteristic property that depends only on the material and the type of phase change (fusion or vaporisation), not on the mass or temperature change.

Official Answer

Q2. (b) energy is absorbed and temperature remains constant

During melting, ice absorbs latent heat of fusion (336 J g⁻¹) from the surroundings to break the rigid lattice bonds. Since all this energy goes into changing the phase (breaking bonds) rather than increasing kinetic energy, the temperature remains constant at 0°C throughout the melting process.

Official Answer

Q3. (d) 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹

Specific heat capacity c = Thermal capacity / mass = 2.5 / 5 = 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Specific heat capacity is an intensive property — it is a characteristic of the material and does NOT change when mass changes. Whether the mass is 5 g or 15 g (tripled), the specific heat capacity remains 0.5 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Official Answer

Q4. (d) remains the same

Specific heat capacity is an intrinsic (intensive) property of a material. Changing the shape or size of a substance (stretching, cutting, melting) does not alter its specific heat capacity. Therefore, stretching the wire to twice its length leaves the specific heat capacity unchanged.

Official Answer

Q5. (c) latent heat of fusion

Latent heat of fusion is the total heat energy required to melt a given mass of a substance at its melting point without any change in temperature. It equals mass × specific latent heat (Q = mL). The 'specific' latent heat of fusion is the heat per unit mass. 'Latent heat of fusion' applies to the given mass, making (c) correct.

Official Answer

Q6. (b) Substance B

Since all substances have the same mass and heat is absorbed at the same rate, the substance that takes the longest time to melt absorbs the most heat. Q = mL, so longer time → more heat absorbed → higher specific latent heat L. B takes 7 minutes (the longest), so B has the highest specific latent heat.

Official Answer

Q7. (a) Substance A will experience a smaller temperature change than substance B

From Q = mcΔT → ΔT = Q/(mc). Since Q and m are the same for both, ΔT is inversely proportional to c. Substance A has higher c, so it will have a smaller temperature rise (ΔT_A < ΔT_B).

Q8. The physical quantity measured in calories is heat (thermal energy).

Relation: 1 calorie = 4.2 joules (or more precisely 4.186 J).

One calorie is defined as the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1°C (from 14.5°C to 15.5°C). The S.I. unit of heat is the joule (J).

Q10. Since Q = mcΔT, and both P and Q receive the same heat Q and rise by the same temperature ΔT:

For P: Q = m_P × c_P × ΔT
For Q: Q = m_Q × c_Q × ΔT

Therefore: m_P × c_P = m_Q × c_Q

c_P/c_Q = m_Q/m_P = 1/2

The ratio of specific heat capacities c_P : c_Q = 1 : 2.
The specific heat capacity of Q is twice that of P.

Q11. No, the specific heat capacity of a substance does NOT necessarily remain the same when its state changes from solid to liquid.

Example: Ice has a specific heat capacity of 2100 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (2.1 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹), whereas water (the liquid state of ice) has a specific heat capacity of 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹) — exactly double. The molecular arrangement and intermolecular forces change during a phase change, altering the energy needed per degree of temperature change.

C. Short Answer Questions

Q12. Principle of Calorimetry: In an isolated system, the heat lost by the hot body equals the heat gained by the cold body.
Heat lost = Heat gained

Material used for calorimeter: Copper (sometimes polished copper or copper-aluminium alloy).

Characteristic properties of copper suitable for calorimeters:
1. Low specific heat capacity (390 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) — minimises heat absorbed by the calorimeter itself, reducing experimental error.
2. Good thermal conductivity — allows quick and even distribution of heat within the calorimeter, enabling rapid thermal equilibrium.
3. Copper is non-reactive with most substances used in experiments.

Q14. The cooling curve for naphthalene from 100°C to 30°C has three regions:

Region PQ (100°C → 80°C): Liquid naphthalene cools from 100°C to its freezing point 80°C. Temperature falls steadily.

Region QR (horizontal at 80°C): Naphthalene solidifies (freezes) at constant temperature of 80°C. During this period, naphthalene releases its latent heat of fusion to the surroundings. The temperature remains constant at 80°C until all the liquid has solidified. This is the FREEZING region.

Region RS (80°C → 30°C): Solid naphthalene cools from 80°C to room temperature (30°C). Temperature falls steadily.

Note: The slope of PQ is steeper than RS if the specific heat capacity of liquid naphthalene is less than that of solid naphthalene.

Q15. (a) Parts of the heating curve:
- AB: Temperature of the solid rises from its initial temperature to its melting point t₁ (sensible heat absorbed; slope depends on specific heat capacity of solid).
- BC: Horizontal line at melting point t₁. The solid melts at constant temperature. Latent heat of fusion is being absorbed to break down the crystal lattice.
- CD: Temperature of the liquid rises from melting point t₁ to boiling point t₂ (sensible heat absorbed by liquid).
- DE: Horizontal line at boiling point t₂. The liquid vaporises at constant temperature. Latent heat of vaporisation is being absorbed to break intermolecular bonds.

(b) The melting point of the substance is the temperature corresponding to the horizontal portion BC.

(c) The boiling point of the substance is the temperature corresponding to the horizontal portion DE.

(d) The slope of CD is less than that of AB because the specific heat capacity of the liquid state (CD) is higher than that of the solid state (AB). A higher specific heat capacity means more heat is needed per degree rise in temperature, so the temperature rises more slowly — resulting in a less steep (smaller) slope on the graph.

Q = mL
L = Q/m = 24,000 / 0.150 = 160,000 J kg⁻¹ = 1.6 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹

Q18. Given: m_water = 150 g, m_cal = 50 g, T_initial = 32°C, T_final = 5°C
c_water = 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹, c_cal = 0.4 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹, L_ice = 330 J g⁻¹; ice at 0°C

Heat released by water and calorimeter cooling from 32°C to 5°C:
Q_water = 150 × 4.2 × 27 = 17,010 J
Q_cal = 50 × 0.4 × 27 = 540 J
Q_total = 17,550 J

Heat absorbed by ice (melting + warming to 5°C):
Q_ice = m × L + m × c_water × 5 = m × 330 + m × 4.2 × 5 = 330m + 21m = 351m

Setting equal:
351m = 17,550
m = 17,550 / 351 = 50 g

Answer: Mass of ice needed = 50 g

Q19. Given: m_water = 104 g, T_initial = 30°C, T_final = 10°C
m_copper = 42 g, c_copper = 0.4 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹
c_water = 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹, L_ice = 336 J g⁻¹; ice at 0°C

Heat given by water:
Q_water = 104 × 4.2 × (30 − 10) = 104 × 4.2 × 20 = 8736 J

Heat given by copper calorimeter:
Q_copper = 42 × 0.4 × 20 = 336 J

Total heat given = 8736 + 336 = 9072 J

Heat taken by ice (melting + warming from 0°C to 10°C):
Q_ice = mL + mc_water × 10 = 336m + 4.2m × 10 = 336m + 42m = 378m

By principle of calorimetry:
378m = 9072
m = 9072 / 378 = 24 g

Answer: Mass of ice added = 24 g

Q26. Given: m_ice = 280 g, T_ice = 0°C; m_water = 600 g, T_water = 50°C
m_container = 300 g; T_final = 10°C; L_ice = 336 J g⁻¹, c_water = 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹
Let specific heat capacity of container = c J g⁻¹ K⁻¹

Heat gained by ice:
Q_ice = m × L + m × c_water × (10 − 0)
= 280 × 336 + 280 × 4.2 × 10
= 94,080 + 11,760 = 105,840 J

Heat lost by water:
Q_water = 600 × 4.2 × (50 − 10) = 600 × 4.2 × 40 = 100,800 J

Heat lost by container:
Q_container = 300 × c × (50 − 10) = 300c × 40 = 12,000c

By principle of calorimetry (heat gained = heat lost):
105,840 = 100,800 + 12,000c
12,000c = 5,040
c = 5,040 / 12,000 = 0.42 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹

Answer: Specific heat capacity of metal container = 0.42 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (= 420 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
