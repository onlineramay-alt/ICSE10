Q9. (a) The phenomenon is Resonance. The natural frequency of wire B is exactly equal to the frequency of the tuning fork (300 Hz). When the vibrating tuning fork is placed on box B, it drives the wire B at its natural frequency. At resonance, the wire B vibrates with a very large amplitude, which is enough to throw off the paper rider placed on it.

(b) 'f' is not equal to 300 Hz; f is greater than 300 Hz (or less than 300 Hz — i.e., f ≠ 300 Hz). Justification: The paper rider on wire A does not fall off, which means wire A does not resonate with the 300 Hz tuning fork. Therefore, the natural frequency of wire A is different from 300 Hz. Since wire A has a different cross-sectional area from wire B (which resonates at 300 Hz) but the same length and tension, and frequency is inversely proportional to radius (f ∝ 1/r), wire A must have a different (unequal) natural frequency. Since both boxes have different cross-sections, wire A has f ≠ 300 Hz.

Q24. Resonance occurs when a body is acted on by a periodic force whose frequency is exactly equal to the natural frequency of the body, so that the amplitude of vibration becomes very large. To demonstrate it, mount two identical tuning forks of the same frequency on separate sound boxes with their open ends facing each other. Set one fork vibrating and then stop it with the hand; the second fork is found to be vibrating and can be heard sounding, because the periodic pressure changes from the first fork arrived at exactly its natural frequency. Resonance can be a nuisance when marching soldiers cross a bridge, since if their step matches the natural frequency of the bridge the amplitude may build up dangerously, which is why they are ordered to break step.
