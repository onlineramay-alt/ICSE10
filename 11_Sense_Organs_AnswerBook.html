Q39. Colour vision is the function of the cone cells of the retina, but cones need fairly bright light to function and are inactive in dim light. In dim light, only the rod cells (which contain rhodopsin) are active, and rods cannot distinguish colour — they only register the presence and intensity of light, in shades of grey. Hence colours cannot be distinguished in dim light.

Q47. The power of accommodation is the ability of the eye to adjust the focal length of its lens so as to focus on both near and distant objects. The ciliary muscles, which are attached to the lens by suspensory ligaments, regulate the lens's curvature and thereby alter its focal length.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q48. The iris regulates the size of the pupil — it constricts the pupil in bright light (to reduce the light entering) and dilates it in dim light (to allow more light in) — thereby controlling the amount of light that reaches the retina.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q49. Sound waves are collected by the pinna and travel down the auditory canal to strike and vibrate the tympanum (eardrum). These vibrations are transmitted and amplified by the three ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) and passed on through the oval window (fenestra ovalis) to the fluid of the cochlea. The resulting fluid vibrations stimulate the sensory hair cells of the Organ of Corti, which generate nerve impulses that travel via the auditory nerve to the brain.

Model Answer (not an official mark scheme)

Q50. For viewing distant objects, the ciliary muscles relax, which increases the tension on the suspensory ligaments; this pulls the lens flatter/thinner and increases its focal length. For viewing near objects, the ciliary muscles contract, slackening the suspensory ligaments; the lens's own elasticity then lets it bulge and become thicker/more rounded, decreasing its focal length.
