Q24. The prism periscope forms a brighter image. In the prism periscope, light undergoes total internal reflection at the glass-air interface, and 100% of the incident light is reflected with no energy loss. In the mirror periscope, only about 95% of light is reflected (some is absorbed and some transmitted through the glass), resulting in a dimmer image. Since TIR involves no energy loss, the prism periscope produces a brighter image.

Official Answer

Q25. (a) The angle of incidence at surface AB = 0° (the ray is incident normally on AB, i.e., it enters without bending).
(b) Since TIR occurs at the inner surface of BC, the angle of incidence there must equal the critical angle. Given the geometry (right-angle prism with 45°-45°-90° angles), the ray hits BC at 45°. Therefore, the critical angle = 45°.
(c) If temperature increases, the refractive index of glass decreases (glass expands, density decreases, speed of light in glass increases). Since sin C = 1/µ, a smaller µ gives a larger C. Therefore, the critical angle increases.

Official Answer

Q26. At minimum deviation, the ray travels symmetrically through the prism: the angle of incidence at the first surface equals the angle of emergence at the second surface. The refracted ray inside the prism is parallel to the base.

Two factors on which angle of deviation depends:
(i) The angle of incidence at the prism face — as the angle of incidence changes, the angle of deviation changes.
(ii) The refractive index (material) of the prism — a prism of higher refractive index produces greater deviation.
(iii) The angle of the prism (apex angle) — a larger apex angle produces greater deviation. (Any two of the three.)

Q28. A right-angled isosceles prism (45°-45°-90°) can erect an inverted image by turning the image through 180° using two total internal reflections.

The inverted image acts as the object. Light rays from it enter the hypotenuse face of the prism normally. They strike the first 45° face and undergo TIR, travel across the prism, strike the second 45° face and undergo TIR again. The rays then exit through the hypotenuse. The image is now rotated 180° and appears erect.

This prism is used in binoculars and in Galilean telescopes as an image erector.
